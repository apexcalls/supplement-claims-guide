Do Supplement Brands Need to Prove Their Claims?

Supplement bottles make bold claims, but the rules behind those claims are often misunderstood. In many countries, supplements are regulated as food, not as medicines. That means a brand does not usually have to prove a product works before selling it, in the way a drug maker must. Understanding that gap is essential for any buyer who wants to judge claims fairly.

The first thing to know is what a claim actually is. A structure or function claim describes how an ingredient supports normal body function, such as supporting immunity or energy. It is not a promise to treat a disease. Brands may use such claims if they have some evidence, but the evidence standard is lower than for medicines, and regulators rarely check every claim before sale.

The second thing is who checks. Regulators act mainly after a product is on sale, responding to complaints or safety reports. That post-market system means a misleading claim can circulate for months before anyone challenges it. A buyer cannot rely on the fact that a product is on sale as proof that its claims have been verified.

The third thing is what evidence looks like. Good evidence is a published study in people, using the same ingredient, form and dose as the product. Weak evidence is a study in cells or animals, a study of a different ingredient, or a tradition of use with no modern testing. Many labels cite research, but few tell you whether the research actually matches the product in the bottle.

So how should a buyer judge a claim? Start by asking three questions. Is the claim specific, or vague? Does the brand name the study or the ingredient form? Is the dose on the label the dose used in the study? A brand that answers all three clearly is easier to trust than one that relies on slogans and testimonials.

No review can guarantee a product works for you, but a claim that survives independent checking is far stronger than one that only appears in advertising.
